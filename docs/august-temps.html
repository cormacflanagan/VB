August on the Santa Cruz sand

The average of every daily high temperature in August, one number per year, back to 1893. 124 Augusts have a daily record complete enough to average; NOAA’s homogenised version of the same station carries the series to 2025. The town’s Augusts are remarkably level — the long-run mean daytime high is 75.2°F, and the spread from the coolest August to the warmest is under 14°F. Whether the afternoons have warmed at all is a question this station cannot settle.

Each thin line is the year’s own average: add up the daily high for every day the observer recorded in August, divide by the number of days. The heavy line over it is an 11-year centred mean, which is what a climate signal would have to move. Teal is the station record as written down. Why there are two lines

Santa Cruz in August is a marine-layer climate: the ocean sets the daytime ceiling, and on the face of it the ceiling has not moved. Over 124 Augusts the observed trend is −0.03°F per decade, and the last 30 Augusts average −0.5°F against the first 30 — nothing, and the wrong sign for a warming story.

The catch is that the daytime record cannot settle its own sign. Homogenising it — correcting for changes of observation hour, instrument and siting — turns −0.03 into +0.08°F per decade. The correction is larger than the trend it is correcting, so which way August afternoons have gone here is a question this station cannot answer on its own.

That the raw line carries station history is not a guess. Differenced against Watsonville, 21 km down the coast, the August daytime gap has held three different levels: near +5°F through the 1940s to the 1970s, near +1.5°F from 1980 to 2008, and near +4°F again since. Every August from 2009 to 2015 sits above the whole 2000–2008 range, and the same jump shows against Watsonville’s airport station and San Jose’s. It is daytime-only — the nighttime gap does not move — and warm-season-only, which is the signature of a gauge’s exposure to the sun rather than of a climate.

The station histories say which of the two is the moving part. NOAA’s own metadata has Santa Cruz reading at 8am until 1987, at 5pm from 1987 to 2017, and at 7pm from 2018; relocated 0.54 miles west-southwest in 2017 after the previous observer gave up, and at least three times before that. It is a backyard gauge — the site description records a house 8 to 35 feet away and a fence at five. Watsonville has not moved since 1941 and has read at 8am since 1931.

NOAA’s algorithm reads the same evidence the other way round, and that is worth sitting with. Its largest correction at this station is +2.5°F applied to 1999–2008 — it concludes that stretch reads too cool, not that the years after it read too warm. Given the century-long relationship with Watsonville, that is the more defensible call. It also puts the size of all this in proportion: the correction on 2015 is −1.4°F, not four. A 4-to-6-degree gap between two stations is a change in their difference, not an error bar on a thermometer.

What NOAA changes, in °F, before the gold line on the chart above is drawn. The dashed line is the time-of-observation correction, which follows from the hour the observer read the gauge; the solid line adds the pairwise homogenisation on top of it. Flat treads are the algorithm’s own segments — it does not adjust years, it adjusts the stretch between two breaks. Hover any year for its two components.

So 2015 at 84.0°F stays the warmest August in this record even after adjustment, at 82.6°F. What changes is how exceptional it looks: the 2000s come up by +1.9°F and 2015 comes down, closing about three degrees of the gap between them.

The nights are the other half of the question, and unlike the afternoons they give a clear answer: August nights warmed 4.7°F relative to the days, and that trend survives the same correction that erases this one.

How it is computed, and what it is not

  • The average is of daily highs, not of daily temperature. Each year’s number is the mean of TMAX over the days of August — the afternoon peak. The other element, and the daily average, are different series and move differently: that is the point of having both pages.
  • One station, because there is only one. Santa Cruz, California, USC00047916, at 36.9878°N 121.9994°W and 21.3 m, a US Historical Climatology Network site reporting daily since 1893. Every other Santa Cruz entry in GHCN is a modern volunteer rain gauge with no thermometer. Its own microclimate is the town’s, not the beach’s: a sea-cliff site a mile from the wharf, cooler than inland Scotts Valley and warmer than the water’s edge.
  • Days that failed NOAA’s quality control are dropped — 4 of them across the whole August record for this element — and a year needs 28 of 31 days to be averaged at all. Averaging a half-observed month against a full one compares two different things.
  • Reliability runs out at both ends, differently. Before about 1900 the observing hour was less standardised, which is exactly what homogenisation exists to correct: read the gold line there. After April 2022 the station simply stopped, so 2021 is the last year with an observed value, and the 4 years past it — 14 August values in total — are inference from neighbours rather than measurement here.
  • The trend figures are ordinary least squares on the annual means, in °F per decade: −0.031 observed over 124 Augusts, +0.078 homogenised over 133. Neither is a forecast, and August alone says nothing about the rest of the year.
