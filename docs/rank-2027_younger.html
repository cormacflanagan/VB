Four corrections, all rejected

The previous version of this page ranked Haleigh Bauer twelfth on a record of 100–14 containing three matches against anyone at 7.5 or better, two thirds of them with one partner. That is a fair objection, and the obvious answer — pull back ratings resting on soft evidence — was tried four ways. Held-out matches rejected all four.

CorrectionHeld-out log-lossResult
Deduct k standard errors0.6073 → 0.6313 worse at every k > 0
Steepen the scale for large gaps0.6073 → 0.6240 worse at every setting
Add habitual opponent strength0.6073 → 0.6259 worse, while improving training fit
Blame estimation noise— same bias in precise and imprecise teams

The third is the sharpest. Adding a player's habitual level of opposition to the model improves training log-loss from 0.4456 to 0.4321 and makes held-out prediction monotonically worse — textbook overfitting. Once a rating is known, the level she earned it against adds nothing. Splitting held-out matches by how lopsided each side's record was, with match count held fixed, likewise finds no bias. So the rating is not demonstrably too high, and this page does not pretend it has fixed something it has not.

What is true instead

The objection was right about where to look and wrong about what it would find there. The problem is not that Ella Olson is too high; it is that her place in this list is barely determined at all. Across the forty refits she lands anywhere from 5 to 87. Brooklyn Rosales, one row below her, moves only from 7 to 35; Thais Treumann at the top moves from 1 to 1. Same cohort, same method, three wildly different amounts of evidence — and a plain sorted list shows none of it.

The whisker is two standard errors either side of the rating; marked dots are players whose standard error falls in the top fifth of the cohort. The rank range in the table below is computed differently and matters more: the 5th-to-95th percentile of the places a player actually took across the refits, ranks recomputed inside each one. That captures errors moving together — a player and her regular partner especially — which a standard error taken one player at a time cannot.

Everyone is overrated a level up

This is the one real bias, and it applies to every player on the page rather than only to those with soft schedules. Held-out matches, oriented so the side playing furthest above its usual opposition comes first: it wins far less often than the model says, and the shortfall grows monotonically with the size of the step up.

In the top quintile of step-ups the model predicts 18.0% and the actual rate is 11.9%, across 3,664 matches. It survives every control: it is the same size for precisely and imprecisely determined teams, unchanged when the standings-derived pairs are dropped, and curvature in the scale removes only part of it while making prediction worse overall. It is a limit of summing a doubles player up in one number, not a bug with a setting. The practical consequence is that a rating should be read as a summary of results achieved, not as a forecast of how someone would fare a level above what she has played.

Notes

  • Duplicate accounts are merged first. Players re-register, and the site then holds two profiles splitting one career. 171 pairs were merged — same name, class and state, and never once at the same tournament, which two different girls on one regional circuit could not manage. Five collisions failed that test and were left alone. Without this the cut listed Ashley Ruschill twice, at #44 and #55.
  • The minimum-match threshold is gone. The previous version required 20 observations, which admitted a 400-match record against nobody and would have excluded a 15-match record against the best players in the country. Intervals do that job properly; a count never did.
  • Standard errors are measured, not assumed. The whole model is refit 40 times on results resampled by tournament-division — whole draws at a time, since six pool results out of one bracket are not six independent facts — across 50,808 divisions.
  • This rating is not the sharper of the two. On the 2,998 held-out matches where both rate all four players and both have seen the data, TruVolley scores 0.340 log-loss and 0.853 accuracy against this fit's 0.406 and 0.839 — 0.065 of a log-loss point behind. Out of sample the gap is wider still, but there TruVolley is quoted as of today and has already absorbed the matches being predicted, which is why the figures above let this fit see them too. The case for this rating is that it is transparent and adjustable, and that it reaches the players TruVolley has no number for — not that it predicts better.
  • Ratings sit on the TruVolley scale by a quantile map, which is monotone and reorders nobody.
