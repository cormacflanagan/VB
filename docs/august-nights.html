August nights in Santa Cruz

The average of every daily low temperature in August, one number per year, back to 1893. 123 Augusts have a daily record complete enough to average; NOAA’s homogenised version of the same station carries the series to 2025. The long-run mean is 51.7°F — and unlike the afternoons, it has moved. The last 30 Augusts in the record average 4.8°F warmer at night than the first 30, a trend that survives every correction NOAA applies to the same station.

One August, one number

Each thin line is the year’s own average: add up the daily low for every day the observer recorded in August, divide by the number of days. The heavy line over it is an 11-year centred mean, which is what a climate signal would have to move. Teal is the station record as written down. Gold is NOAA’s homogenised version of the same station — the record corrected for the things that shift a thermometer’s reading without the weather shifting: a change in the hour of observation, a move across the yard, a new screen. It runs to 2025 because it fills the years after the station closed from neighbouring sites.

As observed — daily record, 1893–2021 NOAA homogenised — monthly, 1893–2025 Thin: single year. Heavy: 11-year centred mean.

Hover any year for its numbers. Gaps in a line are gaps in the record, not zero: 1930 has no August daily data at all, and 5 further Augusts — 1903, 1925, 1961, 2017, 2020 — were observed on fewer than 28 of their 31 days, marked by a tick on the axis and left out of the mean.

The trend that survives the correction

Nights are where the signal is. Over 123 Augusts the observed trend is +0.46°F per decade, and the last 30 Augusts average +4.8°F warmer at night than the first 30 — 50.0°F against 54.9°F. That is not a subtle effect: on the chart it is the one thing you do not need the heavy line to see.

And it holds up. Homogenising the series — correcting for changes of observation hour, instrument and siting — moves +0.46 to +0.35°F per decade. The correction changes the size of the answer, not the answer. That is the difference from the daytime series, where the same correction is larger than the trend and flips its sign.

The neighbour test does not dent it either. Differenced against Watsonville, 21 km down the coast, the August nighttime gap wanders across 4.0°F over a century — both of these are real stations, tended by hand, and neither is a reference standard — but it wanders without direction, and the warming does not. Every decade average from the 1980s on is warmer than every decade average before it: 52.9°F at the coolest, against 51.4°F at the warmest of the 1890s–1970s. A drift that jitters cannot make a step like that.

Santa Cruz minus Watsonville (USC00049473, 21 km down the coast), mean August daily low, by decade. Decades with fewer than three comparable Augusts are left out. Two stations this close share their weather, so this difference should be flat; the widest decade is marked.

What this cannot separate is the town from the climate. Homogenisation catches the thermometer moving; it does not catch a city growing around a thermometer that stayed put. Santa Cruz went from a town of a few thousand when this record opened to a city of sixty thousand, and built surfaces release at night what they absorbed by day — the textbook shape of an urban heat island is warmer nights with unchanged afternoons, which is exactly the shape here. A warming ocean pushes the same way. The record tells you August nights in this town are 4.8°F warmer than they were; it does not tell you how much of that is the town.

Either way the day–night gap has closed. Across the 112 Augusts with both readings, the mean spread between the day's high and its low fell from 24.4°F over the first 30 to 19.7°F over the last 30, −0.50°F per decade — about a fifth of it gone. Read that with the caveat from August days: part of the daytime half of that gap is the station rather than the sky.

How it is computed, and what it is not

  • The average is of daily lows, not of daily temperature. Each year’s number is the mean of TMIN over the days of August — the pre-dawn low. The other element, and the daily average, are different series and move differently: that is the point of having both pages.
  • One station, because there is only one. Santa Cruz, California, USC00047916, at 36.9878°N 121.9994°W and 21.3 m, a US Historical Climatology Network site reporting daily since 1893. Every other Santa Cruz entry in GHCN is a modern volunteer rain gauge with no thermometer. Its own microclimate is the town’s, not the beach’s: a sea-cliff site a mile from the wharf, cooler than inland Scotts Valley and warmer than the water’s edge.
  • Days that failed NOAA’s quality control are dropped — 25 of them across the whole August record for this element — and a year needs 28 of 31 days to be averaged at all. Averaging a half-observed month against a full one compares two different things.
  • Reliability runs out at both ends, differently. Before about 1900 the observing hour was less standardised, which is exactly what homogenisation exists to correct: read the gold line there. After April 2022 the station simply stopped, so 2021 is the last year with an observed value, and the 4 years past it — 8 August values in total — are inference from neighbours rather than measurement here.
  • The trend figures are ordinary least squares on the annual means, in °F per decade: +0.464 observed over 123 Augusts, +0.355 homogenised over 133. Neither is a forecast, and August alone says nothing about the rest of the year.
